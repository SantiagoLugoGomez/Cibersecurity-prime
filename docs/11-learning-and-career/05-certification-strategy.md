# Certification strategy

> [!TIP]
> **In short:** a certification is evidence of skill you already have, not a substitute for building it. The strategic questions are **which** certification actually signals what a given role needs, **when** to sit it (after the matching hands-on practice, not before), and **how** to sequence a small number of them deliberately rather than collecting many shallowly. This page is the strategy; the specific, currently priced certification list, kept current because prices and exam codes change, lives in [Phase 4 of the roadmap](../../roadmap/phase-4-professional/README.md#certifications-in-order), which this page deliberately does not duplicate.

## What a certification actually signals, and to whom

| Audience | What they read into a certification |
|---|---|
| **Automated resume filters** | A keyword match; this is the blunt reason certain certifications (CompTIA Security+ especially) appear in so many job postings regardless of role depth |
| **Non-technical recruiters** | A proxy for "this person has validated knowledge," with little ability to distinguish a multiple-choice exam from a 24-hour practical one |
| **Technical hiring managers** | Variable, and often skeptical by default; a practical, hands-on exam (building and exploiting a network, writing a real report) carries far more weight with this audience than a multiple-choice exam, because it is harder to pass without the actual skill |
| **Clients in consulting and audit roles** | Sometimes a contractual requirement, independent of what the individual hiring manager personally thinks of the certification's rigor |

**Practical implication:** the same certification means different things to different readers of your CV. A junior role's automated filter may require Security+ regardless of your actual skill; a technical interview at the same company may weigh a well-documented home lab project far more heavily than the certificate itself. Build for both audiences, not just one.

## Skill first, certificate second

The single most common, most expensive mistake is booking an exam before the matching hands-on practice is genuinely done, then either failing (losing the exam fee and the retake, if any) or passing on memorized patterns without durable skill behind it. The roadmap's phase structure exists specifically so that by the time you reach a certification's suggested point in [Phase 4](../../roadmap/phase-4-professional/README.md), the practical work that certification tests has already been done repeatedly, for its own sake, not as last-minute cramming for an exam. A certification earned this way is a confirmation, not a hope.

## Vendor-neutral versus vendor-specific

| Type | Example | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Vendor-neutral** | CompTIA Security+, practical offensive and defensive certifications from independent training providers | Portable across employers and technology stacks; the right default for building a general, transferable foundation |
| **Vendor-specific** | Microsoft's SC-200, AWS's Security Specialty | Signals direct, immediate usefulness on a specific employer's actual stack; worth prioritizing once you know (or strongly suspect) which ecosystem a target role or employer runs on |

Neither category is categorically better; the right choice depends on what you can already predict about where you are heading. Early in the roadmap, vendor-neutral foundations make sense because you do not yet know which ecosystem you will land in; later, once a track and likely employer type becomes clearer, vendor-specific certifications earn their place.

## Practical exams versus multiple-choice exams

| Property | Multiple-choice (with performance-based questions) | Fully practical (hands-on lab or report-based) |
|---|---|---|
| **What it actually tests** | Recognition and recall of facts and scenarios | The ability to actually perform the task under time pressure |
| **How defensible it is to a skeptical interviewer** | Lower; can be passed through memorized question banks without real skill | Higher; much harder to fake, and the exam itself often mirrors real job tasks closely |
| **Preparation style that works** | Structured study material, practice exams | The hands-on labs and deep-practice sessions this entire roadmap already builds toward |
| **Typical role fit** | Often a baseline filter requirement (government contracts, some corporate policies specifically name a multiple-choice standard) | Strongly preferred signal for hiring managers assessing actual technical capability |

**Prefer practical exams once a role's filter does not specifically require a multiple-choice one.** When a multiple-choice certification is genuinely required (a specific filter, a specific contract requirement), treat it as a necessary box to check efficiently, not as the centerpiece of your actual skill development.

## Sequencing, and why one at a time

Plan roughly one certification every six to twelve months through the degree, each one timed to follow, not lead, the roadmap phase or track that builds its underlying skill. Running two unrelated certification preparations at once (or a certification alongside a brand-new CTF focus alongside a new personal project) tends to produce shallow progress on all three rather than real progress on any one, the same cost of context-switching that shows up throughout [Study load modes](03-study-load-modes.md). Finish one, let the skill actually settle, then move to the next.

## Expiry, renewal and "lifetime" distinctions

Read the fine print on every certification before paying for it: some expire on a fixed cycle and require continuing education or a renewal fee to keep current (common for vendor and management certifications); some have both a standard and an enhanced version with different validity periods (a well-known offensive security certification, for instance, has a base version with no expiry and an enhanced version that expires after a fixed number of years); and letting an expired certification quietly sit on a CV without noting its status can look worse in an interview than having it at all. Decide, before paying, whether you are prepared to maintain a certification's renewal requirements for as long as you intend to list it as current.

## Student and regional discounts, as a category

Across the whole certification landscape, three categories of discount recur often enough to always check for before paying full price:

| Discount category | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Academic or student plans** | Many training platforms and some certification bodies offer a materially reduced price with a valid student email or enrollment verification |
| **Regional or emerging-market pricing** | Some certification bodies price differently by country; Colombia specifically qualifies for at least one major vendor's reduced regional voucher program |
| **Bundled training-plus-exam packages** | Often cheaper than buying training and the exam attempt separately, and sometimes include a free retake, which matters given the real possibility of not passing on the first attempt |

The specific, current prices and eligibility details for each of these change often enough that this page deliberately does not list them; [Phase 4 of the roadmap](../../roadmap/phase-4-professional/README.md#certifications-in-order) is kept as the single, dated source for that detail, checked and updated rather than duplicated in a second place that would silently go stale.

## A note on certification treadmills

It is possible to spend years and a meaningful amount of money collecting certifications that never quite convert into a stronger portfolio, a better job, or deeper actual skill, because each one was pursued as an end in itself rather than as confirmation of work already done. The corrective is the same discipline as everywhere else in this knowledge base: a certification is one output of a track of real, documented, practiced skill (see [Portfolio and writing](06-portfolio-and-writing.md)), not a substitute for building that track in the first place.

## Practice

1. Pick the certification that [Phase 4 of the roadmap](../../roadmap/phase-4-professional/README.md#certifications-in-order) suggests as your likely first practical certification, and honestly assess, against your own portfolio and lab work so far, whether the underlying skill is already solid enough that the exam would mostly confirm it rather than require last-minute cramming.
2. Find the current student, regional, or bundled discount available for that same certification, and compute the real price you would actually pay.
3. Read that certification's own current syllabus or exam objectives document, and map each objective back to a specific page or exercise in this knowledge base or the roadmap where you already practiced it.
4. Write a one-paragraph answer, as if in an interview, to "why this certification, and why now," using your own actual portfolio evidence rather than a generic justification.

These support [Module 4](../../roadmap/phase-4-professional/README.md) of the roadmap.

## What to do with this

- [ ] Explain why a practical exam generally carries more weight with a technical interviewer than a multiple-choice one, and when a multiple-choice one is still the right choice anyway.
- [ ] Choose your next certification only after confirming the matching hands-on practice is genuinely done, not before.
- [ ] Check every certification's expiry and renewal terms before paying, and decide in advance whether you will maintain it.
- [ ] Find and apply whichever discount category applies to your situation before paying full price.
- [ ] Avoid preparing for more than one certification at a time.
