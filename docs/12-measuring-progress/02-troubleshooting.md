# Troubleshooting

> [!TIP]
> **In short:** [the study system](../11-learning-and-career/02-the-study-system.md) already gives you a routine for being stuck on a single technical problem. This page is for the harder, less specific kind of stuck: motivation that has quietly evaporated, a resource that is not working for you, a plateau where effort no longer seems to produce progress, or the creeping feeling that everyone else is further ahead. These are normal, they have recognizable patterns, and most of them have a specific, learnable fix rather than requiring raw willpower to push through.

## How to use this page

Find the symptom closest to what you are actually experiencing, read its likely cause, and try the fix before assuming the roadmap itself, or your own ability, is the problem. Most of these symptoms are common enough that they have a name in the learning and motivation research, which is itself useful: a named, understood problem feels far less like a personal failing than an unnamed one.

## "I keep skipping the deep-practice session"

| Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|
| The session has no fixed time on your calendar, so it competes with everything else in the moment | Put it in your calendar as a real appointment, exactly as [the study system](../11-learning-and-career/02-the-study-system.md#protecting-the-habit) recommends; a decision made once in advance beats a decision remade under pressure every week |
| The session always starts with "what should I even do," which creates friction before you have even begun | Decide the specific task the night before, in one sentence, so starting requires no decision at all |
| You are in a Yellow or Red week and have not consciously switched modes | Explicitly declare your mode for the week using [Study load modes](../11-learning-and-career/03-study-load-modes.md), rather than silently failing to do the Green-mode plan |
| The session has felt unproductive recently, so you unconsciously avoid it | Check whether you are doing genuine deep practice (an open problem, from the method, not a walkthrough) or something closer to guided practice that belongs in a different session |

## "I finished the material but I do not feel like it stuck"

This is usually the overconfidence or illusion-of-competence pattern described in [How to measure progress](01-how-to-measure-progress.md), surfacing honestly. The fix is not to reread the material again, which is exactly the low-value technique that produced the fluent-but-shallow feeling in the first place; it is to run a genuine retrieval test (the check-yourself questions, cold, or the self-interview exercise) and let the specific gaps it reveals tell you exactly what to restudy, rather than restudying everything out of general anxiety.

## "I am comparing myself to people who seem way ahead"

| Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|
| Social comparison against people whose starting point, available time, or specific focus you do not actually know | Compare your current self only against your own past self, using the tracking in [How to measure progress](01-how-to-measure-progress.md); it is the only comparison with a controlled baseline |
| Visible achievements (a certification, a CTF win) without visible process (the months of unglamorous practice behind it) | Remember that your own portfolio will eventually show the same visible achievements without showing the process either; what you are comparing is someone's highlight reel against your own full, honest timeline |
| A genuinely faster-moving peer in one specific area, mistaken for being ahead in everything | Identify specifically what they are actually ahead in, and whether it is even on your own track; most "ahead" turns out to be narrower than it first appears |

This feeling has a name, impostor syndrome in its more acute form, and it is reported commonly even among experienced practitioners in this field; its existence is not evidence that you do not belong, it is evidence that you are paying attention to a genuinely hard field.

## "I have been putting in the hours, but I do not feel like I am improving"

This is a **plateau**, and it is a normal, expected part of skill development, not a sign the method has stopped working. It commonly means one of:

| Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|
| You have mastered the current difficulty level and have not noticed it is time to move up | Deliberately attempt something one level harder than your current comfortable practice (a harder machine, a less guided CTF category) and see whether the "stuck" feeling returns, which is itself evidence you were ready to move on |
| You are practicing the same narrow slice repeatedly instead of the varied, interleaved practice described in [Learning science](../11-learning-and-career/01-learning-science.md#interleaving-mix-do-not-block) | Deliberately interleave a different topic or skill into your practice sessions this week |
| Genuine fatigue, not a learning plateau at all | Check whether you are actually in a Yellow or Red week by [Study load modes](../11-learning-and-career/03-study-load-modes.md)'s own definition, and switch modes honestly rather than pushing through burnout disguised as a learning problem |

## "This resource (a course, a book, a platform) is not working for me"

Not every resource suits every learner equally well, and continuing to grind through one that genuinely is not landing is often worse than switching. Before switching, though, rule out the more common underlying cause: are you reading passively instead of using the active techniques in [the study system](../11-learning-and-career/02-the-study-system.md#r-read-and-study-75-min)? If the active techniques are genuinely in place and a specific resource still is not working after a fair attempt, switch to an alternative covered in the same roadmap module rather than assuming the whole topic, or your own ability, is the problem.

## "I do not know if I should deviate from the roadmap's order"

| Situation | Guidance |
|---|---|
| A genuine, strong personal interest pulls you toward a later topic early | A short, bounded detour (a week, not a semester) is fine and often valuable; just return to the planned order afterward rather than letting the detour become a permanent derailment |
| You are stuck on an earlier topic and tempted to skip ahead to avoid the discomfort | Do not; [the study system](../11-learning-and-career/02-the-study-system.md#when-you-get-stuck-and-you-will) already gives you the specific routine for being stuck, and skipping ahead usually just relocates the same gap to a later, more expensive point |
| A course in your actual degree is covering something the roadmap has not reached yet | Follow [Your degree and security](../11-learning-and-career/04-your-degree-and-security.md)'s guidance and let the degree pull the relevant roadmap topic forward, since the two are designed to reinforce each other |

## "I am burned out"

Burnout is different from a plateau or a bad week; it is a sustained depletion that no single technique fixes quickly. The honest, correct response is **Pause mode**, described explicitly in [Study load modes](../11-learning-and-career/03-study-load-modes.md#pause-a-genuine-full-stop), with a written resume plan, not a guilt-driven attempt to push through at the usual intensity. Pushing through burnout tends to produce exactly the overconfident, shallow-learning pattern this knowledge base's methods are designed to avoid, because exhausted study time is low-quality study time regardless of how many hours it logs.

## "I passed a certification or finished a phase, but I do not feel ready"

Revisit the calibration discussion in [How to measure progress](01-how-to-measure-progress.md#the-calibration-problem). Run the objective check: can you answer the phase's mastery checklist items from memory, cold, right now, not just at the moment you first checked them off? If yes, this is very likely ordinary underconfidence, common and not dangerous on its own; if no, you have found a real, fixable gap, and finding it now is strictly better than finding it in an interview.

## "A real security incident, exposure, or ethical gray area came up in my own practice"

This is not a study-habits problem; stop and work through it using [Legal limits and ethics](../01-foundations/05-legal-and-ethics.md) directly, specifically its authorization checklist and gray-area guidance, rather than guessing or improvising under pressure.

## Practice

1. Identify which symptom on this page, if any, currently describes your actual state honestly, not the one that sounds most flattering.
2. Apply its specific fix for two weeks and track, using [How to measure progress](01-how-to-measure-progress.md), whether it actually changed anything measurable.
3. Write a short note to your future self describing which symptom you experienced and what fixed it, so a future recurrence is faster to recognize and address.

These support [the study system](../11-learning-and-career/02-the-study-system.md) and [Study load modes](../11-learning-and-career/03-study-load-modes.md).

## What to do with this

- [ ] Recognize at least three of these patterns well enough to name them quickly when they occur, rather than experiencing them as a vague, unnamed sense of failure.
- [ ] Apply the correct, specific fix rather than defaulting to "try harder" for every symptom.
- [ ] Distinguish a genuine plateau from burnout, since they need opposite responses.
- [ ] Keep a short personal log of which symptom-fix pairs actually worked for you, specifically.
- [ ] Know, in advance, that a real legal or ethical question goes to [Legal limits and ethics](../01-foundations/05-legal-and-ethics.md), not to willpower.
