# Phase 4: Professional level

**Duration:** 12 to 24 months, continuing after you start working.
**When:** From about semester 6 until graduation and beyond.
**Prerequisites:** Phase 3 complete, with a chosen primary track.

This phase is about evidence: certifications that prove practical skill, projects that show you can build and investigate real systems, bug bounty or CTF results, and a visible presence in the community. Employers in Colombia and abroad hire on that evidence.

## Objectives

1. Earn certifications in a logical order, one at a time, each after the hands-on work that justifies it.
2. Build and publish at least three larger portfolio projects.
3. Submit valid findings to a bug bounty or vulnerability disclosure program, following its rules.
4. Contribute to the community: a talk, a CTF challenge, an open-source contribution or mentoring.
5. Be ready for technical interviews in English and Spanish.

## Certifications in order

Principles:

- **Skill first, certificate second.** Book an exam only when the matching practice in this roadmap is done.
- **One at a time.** Plan roughly one certification every 6 to 12 months during the degree.
- **Prefer practical exams.** Hands-on exams carry more weight with technical hiring managers than multiple-choice ones.
- **Use discounts.** Student plans (HTB Academy), student pricing (TCM Security, Microsoft) and regional pricing (CompTIA) cut costs significantly.
- **Check the exam version and price on the day you buy.** Prices below were checked in October 2026 and are in US dollars unless noted. Figures marked *third-party* come from reseller or review sites because the vendor's page did not show them directly.

## Portfolio projects

Build at least three, published in this repo with a README, a diagram and a writeup.

| Project | Track | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Detection lab:** Wazuh or Splunk Free in the home lab, 10 custom detections mapped to ATT&CK, each tested with a simulated technique | Defensive | Log pipelines, detection logic, ATT&CK fluency |
| **Active Directory attack and defense:** GOAD-Light in the home lab, an attack path documented end to end, with the matching detections and hardening steps | Offensive and defensive | AD internals, reporting, purple team thinking |
| **Mock pentest report:** a full engagement against your own lab targets, delivered as a professional PDF report | Offensive or web | Methodology and writing, which is what clients pay for |
| **Secure code review:** findings and patches for an intentionally vulnerable open-source app | Web | Code reading, root cause analysis, working with developers |
| **Cloud misconfiguration checker:** a Python tool that audits your own AWS or Azure account for a handful of risky settings | Cloud | Cloud APIs, IAM, automation |
| **Threat intelligence brief:** a report on a threat relevant to Colombia or Latin America built only from public sources, with ATT&CK mapping and detection ideas | Defensive or malware | Research, analysis, clear writing in English |
| **Original CTF challenge:** design, build and write the intended solution for a challenge used in a university or community CTF | Any | Deep understanding of a vulnerability class |
| **Risk program for a fictional company:** policy set, risk register and CSF 2.0 profile | GRC | Frameworks applied to a realistic case |

## Bug bounty

### How programs work

- A **bug bounty program** pays for valid vulnerabilities in a defined scope. A **vulnerability disclosure program (VDP)** accepts reports but usually pays nothing; it is still good practice and still requires following the rules.
- The **program policy** is the contract. It defines in-scope assets, out-of-scope assets and vulnerability types, testing restrictions (no denial of service, no social engineering, rate limits), and how researchers are protected when they follow the rules (often called safe harbor).
- You submit a **report**; the platform or company **triages** it, may mark it as a duplicate, informative or not applicable, rates its **severity** and pays according to the program's reward table.
- **Disclosure** happens only when the program allows it, usually after the fix.

| Platform | Notes |
|---|---|

### How to start

1. Finish the Hacker101 CTF and the Web Security Academy practitioner labs first.
2. Read disclosed reports on the platforms to learn what accepted findings and good reports look like.
3. Pick one program with a wide scope and stay with it for weeks. Depth beats jumping between programs.
4. Read the whole policy before sending a single request. Respect scope, rate limits and any testing account rules.
5. Expect duplicates and "not applicable" results at the beginning; treat the first months as training.
6. Write every report as if it were a pentest finding: clear title, impact, reproducible steps, evidence and a suggested fix. See [english/writing-reports.md](../../english/writing-reports.md).

The legal side, including Colombian law and responsible disclosure outside formal programs, is in [legal-and-ethics.md](../../legal-and-ethics.md).

## Community and visibility

- Play CTFs with a team and aim for a top-third finish in a mid-level event.
- Give a short talk at an [OWASP Bogotá](https://owasp.org/www-chapter-bogota/) meetup, or submit to the call for papers of [BSides Colombia](https://sessionize.com/bsides-colombia-2026) or a university event.
- Publish writeups and project posts regularly; quality matters more than frequency.
- Contribute to an open-source security project: documentation fixes, new detection rules, or bug reports count.
- Help newer students: mentoring is the fastest way to find the gaps in your own knowledge.

Full list of events and groups: [community.md](../../community.md).
